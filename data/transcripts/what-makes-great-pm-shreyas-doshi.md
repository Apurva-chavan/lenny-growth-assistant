Lenny Rachitsky: Today I'm talking with Shreyas Doshi, former PM leader at Stripe, Twitter, Google, and Yahoo. Shreyas is one of the most thoughtful voices in product management. Welcome to the show.

Shreyas Doshi: Thanks Lenny, it's great to be here.

Lenny: You've worked with hundreds of PMs across some of the best companies in the world. What separates great PMs from good ones?

Shreyas: The biggest separator is what I call upstream thinking. Most PMs are very good at execution — they write specs, they run sprints, they ship features. But great PMs spend most of their time thinking about whether they're working on the right problems in the first place. They ask: why are we building this? What outcome are we trying to achieve? Is this the highest leverage thing we could be doing? That upstream thinking is rare and incredibly valuable.

Lenny: Can you give an example?

Shreyas: Sure. A good PM gets a request from sales: "We need feature X to close deals." A good PM writes the spec and ships feature X. A great PM asks: why are we losing these deals? Is it really because of feature X, or is it a positioning problem, a pricing problem, a sales process problem? Maybe feature X is the right answer. But maybe there's a better answer that the great PM discovers by going upstream.

Lenny: What other traits define great PMs?

Shreyas: Three more. First, taste. Great PMs have strong opinions about what makes a product good. They can look at a design and immediately feel what's right and wrong about it. Taste is hard to teach but you can develop it by consuming great products obsessively and articulating why they work. Second, influence without authority. PMs don't manage engineers or designers, but they need to get them aligned and motivated. The best PMs do this through clarity of vision and genuine respect for their teammates' craft. Third, comfort with ambiguity. Product work is inherently uncertain. Great PMs make good decisions with incomplete information and don't get paralyzed waiting for certainty.

Lenny: What about the relationship between PMs and engineers?

Shreyas: It's the most important relationship in product. The best PM-engineer relationships I've seen are true partnerships. The PM brings the problem and the context. The engineer brings the solution and the technical creativity. When that partnership works, engineers come up with product ideas that PMs never would have thought of. When it breaks down — usually because the PM is too prescriptive about solutions — you lose that creative energy.

Lenny: What mistakes do you see PMs make most often?

Shreyas: The biggest one is confusing output with outcome. PMs get rewarded for shipping — for launching features, hitting roadmap milestones, clearing the backlog. But shipping is output. Outcome is whether users' lives got better, whether the business metric moved. I've seen teams ship 20 features in a quarter and move no metrics. That's a failure, not a success. Great PMs are ruthlessly focused on outcomes.

Lenny: How should PMs think about prioritization?

Shreyas: I use a framework I call the three levels of product work. Level one is execution — shipping what's on the roadmap. Level two is strategy — deciding what should be on the roadmap. Level three is vision — deciding what the product should become in 3-5 years. Most PMs spend 80% of their time at level one. Great PMs spend significant time at levels two and three. If you're always in execution mode, you're always reacting. You need to create space for strategic thinking.

Lenny: What advice do you have for PMs who want to get to the next level?

Shreyas: Three things. First, develop a point of view on your product's strategy and share it proactively. Don't wait to be asked. Second, build deep user empathy — not just through data, but through direct conversations. Talk to users every week. Third, invest in your writing. The best PMs I know are exceptional writers. Clear writing is clear thinking. If you can't write a crisp one-pager about a problem, you don't understand it well enough yet.

Lenny: Shreyas, this has been incredibly insightful. Where can people follow your thinking?

Shreyas: I'm on Twitter at @shreyas. I share frameworks and observations about product work there regularly.

Lenny: Thank you so much.

Shreyas: Thanks Lenny, this was fun.
