Lenny Rachitsky: Today I'm talking with Rahul Vohra, founder and CEO of Superhuman, about how to measure and find product-market fit. Rahul, welcome to the show.

Rahul Vohra: Thanks Lenny, great to be here.

Lenny: You wrote one of the most read essays in startup history about product-market fit. Let's start from the beginning — what is product-market fit?

Rahul: Product-market fit is when you've built something that a significant number of people want so badly that they'd be very disappointed if it went away. The classic definition from Marc Andreessen is being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market. But I think that's too abstract. The way I think about it is: do you have a core group of users who would be genuinely devastated if your product disappeared tomorrow?

Lenny: And how do you measure that?

Rahul: We use a single survey question: how would you feel if you could no longer use this product? The options are very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, or not disappointed. If more than 40% of your users say they'd be very disappointed, you have product-market fit. Below 40%, you don't. It's a simple, actionable benchmark.

Lenny: Where does the 40% number come from?

Rahul: I analyzed hundreds of startups. Companies that struggled to grow were almost always below 40%. Companies that grew strongly were almost always above 40%. It's not a perfect rule, but it's a remarkably consistent signal. Superhuman was at 22% when we first measured. We were not at product-market fit.

Lenny: That's surprising given how beloved Superhuman is now. What did you do?

Rahul: We did something counterintuitive. We stopped trying to convert the people who said they wouldn't be disappointed. Instead, we focused entirely on the people who said they'd be very disappointed. We asked them: what is the main benefit you get from Superhuman? What type of person do you think would most benefit? We used their language, their words, to reposition the product.

Lenny: What did you find?

Rahul: The people who loved us most were power users — founders, executives, people who lived in email. They loved speed above everything else. So we doubled down on speed. We removed features that slowed things down. We optimized every interaction. Within a few months we went from 22% to 58%.

Lenny: That's a massive jump. What's the key lesson?

Rahul: Don't try to please everyone. Find the people who already love you, understand why they love you, and make the product even better for them. The biggest mistake founders make is trying to convert detractors instead of doubling down on fans. Your fans will tell you exactly what to build. Listen to them.

Lenny: What about retention? How does PMF relate to retention?

Rahul: They're deeply connected. If you have product-market fit, retention is almost automatic. People who'd be very disappointed if your product disappeared are not going to churn. The 40% benchmark is essentially a leading indicator of long-term retention. If you're below 40%, no amount of growth hacking will save you. You'll fill a leaky bucket.

Lenny: What are the biggest mistakes founders make when trying to find PMF?

Rahul: Three things. First, they measure the wrong things — vanity metrics like downloads or signups instead of engagement and disappointment scores. Second, they pivot too quickly before really understanding why their current users love or hate the product. Third, they try to be everything to everyone instead of being everything to someone. Narrow focus is how you find PMF.

Lenny: Any advice for founders who are stuck below 40%?

Rahul: Talk to your most engaged users every single week. Not surveys — actual conversations. Ask them what they'd lose if your product disappeared. Ask them what they use instead. Ask them what would make the product twice as good. The answers will surprise you. PMF is not a moment, it's a process of continuous discovery.

Lenny: Rahul, this has been incredibly valuable. Where can people find your essay?

Rahul: It's on First Round Review — just search "Superhuman product-market fit engine." It has the full survey template and the analysis framework.

Lenny: Thank you so much.

Rahul: Thanks Lenny.
