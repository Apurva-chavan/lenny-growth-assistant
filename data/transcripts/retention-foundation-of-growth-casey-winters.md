Lenny Rachitsky: Today I'm speaking with Casey Winters, one of the most respected growth practitioners in Silicon Valley. He was the first growth hire at Pinterest and later CPO at Eventbrite. Casey, welcome.

Casey Winters: Thanks Lenny, happy to be here.

Lenny: Let's talk about retention. You've said retention is the single most important growth metric. Why?

Casey: Because everything else is downstream of retention. Acquisition, monetization, virality — they all compound on top of retention. If you have poor retention, you're running a business where you constantly have to refill a leaky bucket. You spend money acquiring users and they leave. But if you have strong retention, every new user you acquire adds to a growing base. The math is completely different.

Lenny: How do you define good retention?

Casey: It depends heavily on the product category. For a daily use app like social media or messaging, you want day-30 retention above 25-30%. For a weekly use product like a productivity tool, you want week-8 retention above 40%. For a monthly use product like a financial app, you want month-6 retention above 60%. The key is to benchmark against your category, not some universal standard.

Lenny: What's the most common retention mistake you see?

Casey: Optimizing for activation instead of habit formation. A lot of teams focus on getting users to complete onboarding and hit their "aha moment." That's important, but it's not enough. The real question is: does the user come back the next day? And the day after? You need to build a habit loop, not just a good first experience.

Lenny: How do you build a habit loop?

Casey: Three components. First, a trigger — something that brings the user back. Could be a notification, an email, a social cue, or an internal desire. Second, a variable reward — the user gets something valuable but unpredictable each time they return. Pinterest nailed this with the infinite scroll of new pins. Third, investment — the user puts something into the product that makes it more valuable over time. On Pinterest, every pin you save makes your feed better.

Lenny: What about notification strategies for retention?

Casey: Notifications are the most powerful retention tool and the most abused. The rule is: only send a notification when you have something genuinely valuable to tell the user. If you send notifications just to drive opens, users will turn them off or uninstall. At Pinterest we were very conservative. We only sent notifications when someone repinned your content or followed you — real social signals that users actually cared about.

Lenny: What metrics do you track for retention?

Casey: I look at three things. First, the retention curve — does it flatten out or does it keep declining? A flattening curve means you have a retained core. A declining curve means you have a fundamental product problem. Second, cohort analysis — are newer cohorts retaining better than older ones? If yes, your product improvements are working. Third, the resurrection rate — what percentage of churned users come back? A high resurrection rate means your product has real value even if the habit didn't stick initially.

Lenny: What's the relationship between retention and growth?

Casey: Retention is the foundation, growth is the multiplier. You can have great growth tactics — referral programs, SEO, paid acquisition — but if retention is poor, growth will plateau or reverse. The companies that achieve durable growth almost always have retention as their north star metric. At Pinterest, we didn't focus on growth until we had strong retention signals. That discipline is why Pinterest grew so efficiently.

Lenny: Any tactical advice for improving retention quickly?

Casey: Three things that work. First, improve your onboarding to get users to their core value faster. Every extra step in onboarding is a drop-off point. Second, identify your power users — the top 10% most engaged — and interview them. Find out what they do differently. Then build features that help average users behave more like power users. Third, look at your day-1 to day-7 drop-off. That's usually where you lose the most users. Fix that before anything else.

Lenny: Casey, this has been fantastic. Any final thoughts?

Casey: Retention is not a growth team problem. It's a product problem. If your retention is bad, the answer is not more notifications or better emails. The answer is a better product. Growth teams can optimize around the edges, but only product can fix the core.

Lenny: Thank you Casey.

Casey: Thanks Lenny.
